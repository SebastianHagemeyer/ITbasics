Binary meets Excel

Remember the mind-reading cards from Step Up? The trick is really binary, and a spreadsheet can do it for you. In this module you take the trick apart, rebuild it in a spreadsheet, and write the formula that reads minds.

1. The trick, one more time

Think of a whole number from 1 to 31 and keep it secret. Look at each card. If your number is on it, press the button under that card. Then press Read my mind.

How did it know? Look at the first number on each card you picked. Add them up.

2. Why it works: binary

The first numbers on the cards are 16, 8, 4, 2 and 1. Those are the place values of a 5-bit binary number. Every number from 1 to 31 can be made by adding some of them, and there is only one way to do it.

Each card is one bit. Saying "yes, it's on this card" is a 1. Saying "no" is a 0. So when you pressed the buttons, you were really telling the computer your number in binary.

Card value168421Total
On this card? (bit)10101
Counts as16040121

So 21 in binary is 10101. The cards only ever add up the place values that are switched on.

Check your understanding: A friend says their number is on the 8 card, the 2 card and the 1 card, and no others. What is their number?

8 + 2 + 1 = 11. In binary that is 01011: the 16 and 4 cards are off (0) and the others are on (1).

3. The trick as a spreadsheet

Here is the trick built in a spreadsheet, the same one you will build in Excel. Row 1 holds the first number on each card. Row 2 holds a 1 if your number was on that card and a 0 if it wasn't. Cell G5 works out the answer.

Click a 1 or a 0 in row 2 to flip it, and watch G5 change. Then click G5 and look at the formula bar at the top.

Your answers from the card trick are already loaded into row 2.

4. Why multiply?

Click G5 again. The formula is:

=(B1*B2)+(C1*C2)+(D1*D2)+(E1*E2)+(F1*F2)

In Excel, * means times. Each bracket multiplies a card's number by its 1 or 0:

  • If the card is on: 16 × 1 = 16. The card's number counts.
  • If the card is off: 16 × 0 = 0. The card vanishes from the total.

Then the + signs add up whatever is left. Multiplying by 1 or 0 is a really common spreadsheet move. It works like a light switch for a number.

Key idea: every formula starts with =. Without it, Excel thinks you typed some text and just shows it.

Check your understanding: C1 holds 8 and C2 holds 0. What does =C1*C2 show?

8 × 0 = 0. A 0 in row 2 means the card was off, so it adds nothing. It shows a number (not the text C1*C2) because the formula starts with =.

5. Your turn: write the formula

This spreadsheet is missing its formula. Click G5, type a formula into the formula bar that works out the total, then press Enter.

It gets tested against all 32 ways the cards can be on or off, so typing the answer in as a number won't pass. It has to really work.

Stuck? Get a hint

Start with just the first card: =B1*B2. That's the 16 card times its 1 or 0. Now add the next card on the end with a +, and keep going until all five cards are in.

6. Level up: 8 cards

Add three more cards and the trick gets much bigger. Eight cards are eight bits, which is one byte, and the card numbers double each time: 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1.

All eight on: 128 + 64 + 32 + 16 + 8 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 255. That's 11111111, the biggest number one byte can hold. It's why so many things stop at 255, like each colour in an RGB code.

Six cards (1 to 32) only reach 63. Seven cards (1 to 64) reach 127, which covers 100. So you need 7 bits.

Bonus: the 8-card formula

Write the formula for 8 cards in J5. The long way works. So does a shortcut, if you can find it.

The shortcut

=SUMPRODUCT(B1:I1,B2:I2) multiplies the two rows pair by pair (B1 × B2, C1 × C2, ...) and adds up all the answers. B1:I1 is a range: every cell from B1 to I1. Ranges are how you point at lots of cells at once, and you will use them all the time with SUM, MIN, MAX and AVERAGE.

7. Build it in real Excel

Now prove you can do it in the real thing. This counts as practice for two of your pre-VET skills: Excel and file management.

  1. Open Excel and start a blank workbook.
  2. In A1 type First number on the card. In A2 type Was it on. Double-click the line between the A and B column letters so the words fit.
  3. Type 16, 8, 4, 2, 1 into B1 to F1.
  4. Type a 1 or a 0 into each cell from B2 to F2.
  5. Type TOTAL in G4, then your formula in G5. Press Enter.
  6. Test it on a partner: they pick a number, you set row 2 from their answers, and Excel reads their mind.
  7. Save it in your Excel folder as Surname_Firstname_BinaryTrick_v1.

Evidence

Click G5 so the formula shows in the formula bar. Press Windows + Shift + S, drag a box around the table and the formula bar, then open it in the Snipping Tool. Circle the formula and write what it does. Save it as Surname_Firstname_BinaryTrick_evidence.

Why that file name? A good name says whose it is, what it is and which version it is. Your teacher (and later, your boss) can find it without opening it.

Glossary

Binary
A number system that uses only 1s and 0s. Each place is worth double the one to its right: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16...
Bit
One binary digit: a single 1 or 0. One card in the trick.
Byte
8 bits. Can hold any number from 0 to 255.
Spreadsheet
A grid of cells for storing data and doing calculations. Excel is a spreadsheet program.
Cell
One box in a spreadsheet, where a column and a row cross.
Cell reference
A cell's name: column letter, then row number, like G5.
Formula
A calculation in a cell. Always starts with = and updates when the cells it uses change.
Formula bar
The bar above the grid that shows what is really inside the selected cell.
Range
A block of cells written with a colon, like B1:F1 (every cell from B1 to F1).
SUMPRODUCT
A function that multiplies two ranges pair by pair and adds up the results.
